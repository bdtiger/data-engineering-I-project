\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Application Domain and Scientific Context}
Urban transportation systems are a primary pillar of modern smart cities, generating vast volumes of heterogeneous data that can be leveraged to improve traffic management, infrastructure planning, and mobility services. The domain of urban mobility is increasingly dependent on large-scale data to solve logistical challenges in densely populated areas. One of the most widely used and comprehensive real-world datasets in this domain is the New York City Taxi Trip dataset, published by the New York City Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC) \cite{TLC2024}. This dataset provides a granular view of city-wide movement by recording millions of individual trips, including precise pickup and drop-off timestamps, geographic zones, trip distances, and detailed fare amounts.

\subsection{Dataset Importance and Research Relevance}
The NYC taxi dataset is particularly important for data engineering research because it serves as a high-velocity and high-volume benchmark that mirrors the complexity of real-world "Big Data." Its importance lies not only in its scale but also in its ability to reflect human behavior and urban dynamics. Prior research has demonstrated the utility of taxi trip data in various high-impact applications, such as demand prediction, traffic flow analysis, and anomaly detection. For instance, studies in urban computing have utilized mobility datasets to identify spatial and temporal demand patterns, enabling more efficient resource allocation and reduced carbon emissions in cities \cite{Andrienko2013}. Furthermore, such datasets have been used to explore predictive modeling techniques for trip durations and to detect irregular travel patterns. 

\subsection{Analysis Objective and Engineering Focus}
In this project, the analytical objective is intentionally simple: to compute the average trip duration and average fare amount, grouped by pickup zone and hour of the day. This type of aggregation represents a standard workload in data engineering pipelines, involving essential operations such as filtering, grouping, and joining large datasets. By maintaining a straightforward analysis goal, we ensure that the focus remains on the critical engineering aspects—specifically system architecture, performance optimization, and scalability—rather than algorithmic complexity. This approach aligns with the core principles of the course, which emphasize the "heavy lifting" of data processing over advanced statistical modeling.

\subsection{Distributed Processing and Scalability Framework}
To process this dataset efficiently, the project utilizes a modern big data stack consisting of Apache Spark for distributed computation \cite{Zaharia2016} and the Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS) for scalable storage. These technologies allow for parallel execution across multiple nodes, which is a necessity given that large-scale data challenges have historically been addressed using paradigms like MapReduce \cite{Dean2008}. The system is deployed on a constrained cluster environment consisting of virtual machines, reflecting realistic limitations in computational resources. A central focus of this project is the evaluation of system scalability through both horizontal and vertical scaling experiments. The observed performance results are interpreted using theoretical models such as Amdahl's Law \cite{Amdahl1967}, providing a foundation for understanding the limits of parallel speedup in a distributed system.
