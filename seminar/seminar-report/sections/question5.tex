\section{Question 5: Scalability -- Theory and Practice}

Scalability is a core principle in distributed systems and refers to the ability of a system to handle increasing workloads by proportionally increasing computational resources. From a theoretical perspective, scalability is fundamentally constrained by limits of parallelism. Amdahl’s Law states that the maximum achievable speedup of a parallel system is limited by the fraction of computation that remains sequential. Even with a large number of processors, the sequential component becomes a bottleneck. In contrast, Gustafson’s Law argues that when system size increases, workloads typically grow proportionally, allowing effective utilization of additional processors. This distinction corresponds to the difference between strong scaling, where runtime decreases for a fixed workload, and weak scaling, where workload increases proportionally with resources while runtime remains stable. Empirical studies show that architectural overhead, such as centralized coordination mechanisms, can significantly limit scalability at large scale \cite{lunacek2013}.

From a technological perspective, scalability in cloud systems is mainly achieved through horizontal scaling, where additional virtual machines or containers are deployed dynamically. Early research on auto-scaling classified approaches into threshold-based rules, control theory, reinforcement learning, queuing theory, and time-series analysis \cite{lorido2014}. Threshold-based rules are simple and widely adopted in commercial cloud platforms, but they are purely reactive and may lead to oscillation, over-provisioning, or SLA violations if thresholds are not carefully tuned \cite{lorido2014}. More advanced approaches such as control-theoretic and queuing models attempt to optimize resource allocation by modeling system dynamics, although they require accurate system knowledge and may lack flexibility in highly dynamic environments.

Autoscaling mechanisms can generally be categorized as reactive or proactive. Reactive scaling adjusts resources based on current system metrics such as CPU utilization or request rate. Kubernetes’ Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) is a typical example, scaling pods based on observed resource consumption. While reactive mechanisms are robust and relatively easy to deploy, they suffer from delayed response due to monitoring intervals and container startup times \cite{dogani2023}. In contrast, proactive autoscaling incorporates workload prediction techniques, such as time-series forecasting or machine learning models, to allocate resources before overload occurs. Predictive autoscaling has been shown to reduce response time and improve resource efficiency compared to purely reactive mechanisms \cite{ju2021}. Hybrid approaches combine reactive scale-up with predictive scale-down strategies to reduce SLA violations and avoid oscillatory behavior in cloud infrastructures \cite{ali2012}.

With the rise of container-based virtualization and microservices, scalability has become more fine-grained and dynamic. Containers are lightweight compared to virtual machines and allow faster scaling operations, but they also introduce new orchestration complexities. Recent surveys emphasize that most current container-based autoscaling mechanisms remain reactive and rely heavily on CPU-based thresholds \cite{dogani2023}. However, modern workloads, particularly microservices and machine learning applications, often require multi-resource scaling strategies involving CPU, memory, network bandwidth, and GPU resources. Designing autoscalers for containerized cloud and edge environments therefore requires more sophisticated, multi-metric decision mechanisms \cite{dogani2023}.

Edge and fog computing introduce additional scalability challenges. Unlike centralized cloud data centers, edge environments are heterogeneous and resource-constrained. Workloads are highly dynamic and latency-sensitive, especially in IoT applications. Traditional cloud autoscaling techniques cannot be directly applied without adaptation \cite{dogani2023}. Proactive and hybrid autoscaling strategies become increasingly important in such contexts to prevent SLA violations while minimizing resource waste. However, accurate prediction at the edge is difficult due to limited historical data and variable network conditions.

In conclusion, scalability is theoretically bounded by limits of parallelism but practically enabled through distributed architectures and intelligent resource management. Reactive autoscaling offers simplicity and operational stability, whereas proactive and hybrid strategies improve responsiveness and cost-efficiency. The evolution from VM-based cloud systems to containerized cloud and edge environments demonstrates that scalability is no longer merely a hardware provisioning problem but a dynamic control and prediction problem requiring adaptive, data-driven approaches \cite{lorido2014,dogani2023}.
